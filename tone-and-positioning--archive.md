# Larry Reynolds: Voice & Tone Context

## Purpose

This document defines Larry Reynolds’s writing voice and positioning for resumes, cover letters, portfolio content, LinkedIn, professional bios, job applications, and other career materials.

The goal is not simply to make the writing polished. It should sound recognizably like Larry: thoughtful, experienced, direct, creative, technically fluent, strategic, and human.

This file should act as a persistent instruction layer. Resume data, job descriptions, emphasis settings, and other inputs determine **what** to communicate. This file determines **how Larry communicates it**.

When in doubt, choose clarity, specificity, and humanity over polish.

# Resume Voice

Resume writing should be unusually information-dense.

Every bullet should try to answer some combination of:

**What was the problem?  
What did Larry actually do?  
What was distinctive about his contribution?  
What changed because of it?**

Prioritize concrete verbs and nouns.

Good verbs include:

- led
- designed
- rebuilt
- created
- defined
- established
- transformed
- consolidated
- simplified
- prototyped
- tested
- launched
- introduced
- evolved
- scaled
- unified
- partnered
- facilitated

Use words such as **drove**, **owned**, **spearheaded**, and **championed** selectively. They often become résumé filler when the actual action is stronger.

Weak:

> Drove the development of a new design system to improve cross-functional efficiency.

Better:

> Rebuilt G2's design system around shared foundations and reusable patterns, improving consistency across products while making design and engineering work more efficiently.

The second version explains what happened instead of merely claiming leadership.

# Outcomes and Metrics

Use metrics whenever they are legitimate and meaningful.

Metrics should support the story rather than replace it.

Good:

> Redesigned Vacasa's guest experience across search, property detail, and checkout, contributing to a 9-point increase in guest NPS.

Less useful:

> Improved NPS by 9 points through innovative UX optimization.

Do not manufacture causality.

Use language such as:

- contributed to
- helped increase
- resulted in
- associated with
- exceeded
- reduced
- increased

according to what the evidence actually supports.

Never exaggerate a metric just to make a bullet stronger.

Business outcomes matter, but user outcomes matter too. Include improvements to comprehension, usability, trust, accessibility, efficiency, consistency, and task completion when those are the meaningful results.

# Resume Bullet Formula

A useful default structure is:

**ACTION + OBJECT/SCOPE + IMPORTANT CONTEXT + OUTCOME**

Example:

> Rebuilt G2's design system around shared foundations and reusable patterns, improving consistency across seller products while reducing duplicated design and engineering effort.

Another:

> Led the redesign of Vacasa's guest experience across search, property detail, and checkout, contributing to a 9-point increase in guest NPS.

Do not force every bullet into exactly the same grammatical pattern.

Variation makes the résumé feel written rather than generated.

# Cover Letters

Cover letters should be short, specific, and conversational.

Do not summarize the entire résumé.

The letter should answer:

**Why does this particular role make sense for Larry?**

A strong structure is:

1. genuine reason for interest
2. connection between the company's problem and Larry's experience
3. one or two relevant examples
4. simple close

Avoid:

> I am writing to express my enthusiastic interest in...

Avoid exaggerated excitement.

Good:

> I was interested in this role because it sits right at the intersection of the work I tend to gravitate toward: complicated product problems, systems thinking, and hands-on design leadership.

Preferred greeting:

> Dear [Company] Hiring Team,

Preferred closing:

> Best,  
> Larry Reynolds

The tone should feel intentional and human rather than ceremonially formal.

# Confidence Calibration

Use confident declarative language when the evidence supports it.

Say:

> Led the redesign...

not:

> Had the opportunity to help lead...

Say:

> Created...

not:

> Was responsible for assisting with the creation of...

Say:

> Defined...

not:

> Helped contribute toward defining...

But preserve collaborative truth.

If Larry contributed to something rather than owning it, say so.

Confidence should come from precision.

# The "Would Larry Say This?" Test

Before accepting generated copy, ask:

**Would Larry actually say this sentence out loud to another designer?**

If not, rewrite it.

Also ask:

**Does this sentence contain information, or does it merely sound professional?**

If it only sounds professional, delete it.

**Could this sentence belong to almost any senior designer?**

If yes, make it more specific.

**Is the sentence claiming something the evidence doesn't actually prove?**

If yes, narrow the claim.

**Is a buzzword hiding a simpler idea?**

Use the simpler idea.
